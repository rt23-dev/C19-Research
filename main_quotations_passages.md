# Master Quotations and Passages

Last updated: 2026-05-04

## Purpose

This file gathers the most useful quotations, paraphrased passages, and citable formulations identified so far for the translation-factories project. It is designed as a writing aid: each entry gives the passage, source, date, and why it matters.

## How to use this file

- `Direct quotation` means wording taken directly from a primary or public-domain source now in hand.
- `Reported wording` means wording preserved through a scholarly summary or source-anthology tradition and should be cited with care.
- `Paraphrase` means the source is secure, but the wording here is condensed for note-taking.

## 1. Hauff and the classic factory image

### 1.1 The factory as a production chain

- Type: Direct quotation tradition already cited in project notes
- Source: Wilhelm Hauff, `Die Buecher und die Lesewelt` (1827), as cited in Bachleitner and the Vienna script
- Key wording:
  `In Scheerau hat man jetzt eine eigene Uebersetzungsfabrik angelegt`
- Why it matters:
  This is the canonical naming of the translation factory in literary satire.

- Type: Reported wording
- Source: Hauff via Bachleitner / Vienna script
- Key details:
  `fuenfzehn Menschen` work in each of two halls.
  Each receives `ein halber Bogen von Walter Scott`.
  `Stilisten` revise the rough work.
  Separate `poetische Arbeiter` handle verse and mottos.
- Why it matters:
  This is the fullest compact image of translation as segmented labor.

## 2. Gutzkow and the political economy of translation factories

### 2.1 Root cause: booksellers, not translators

- Type: Paraphrase from direct reading of local scans
- Source: Karl Gutzkow, `Die Deutschen Uebersetzungsfabriken`, `Telegraph fuer Deutschland`, no. 7 (1839), p. 49
- Key wording:
  The evil does not lie in poor hacks who translate out of need, but in `Buchhaendler`, seized by `Speculationswuth`.
- Why it matters:
  This is one of the most important conceptual shifts in the project. Gutzkow locates the problem in the market structure, not only in translator incompetence.

### 2.2 Market flooding and the devaluation of domestic literature

- Type: Paraphrase from direct reading of local scans
- Source: Gutzkow, `Telegraph fuer Deutschland`, no. 7 (1839), pp. 49-50
- Key wording:
  Booksellers `ueberfuellen` the market with foreign wares and `herabdruecken` the value of native literary products.
- Why it matters:
  This is the clearest statement of the translation factory as an economic problem.

### 2.3 National literary humiliation

- Type: Paraphrase from direct reading of local scans
- Source: Gutzkow, `Telegraph fuer Deutschland`, no. 7 (1839), pp. 49-50
- Key wording:
  Booksellers make Germany ridiculous abroad by creating the impression that the nation must defer to foreign writers who often receive little notice at home.
- Why it matters:
  This links translation factories to national-literary anxiety, not just to style or quality.

### 2.4 The Netherlands analogy

- Type: Paraphrase from direct reading of local scans
- Source: Gutzkow, `Telegraph fuer Deutschland`, no. 7 (1839), p. 50
- Key wording:
  If the system continues, Germany will end up like the Netherlands, filling reading demand through imported literature while native literature shrivels.
- Why it matters:
  This is one of the strongest passages for the protectionist side of the argument.

### 2.5 Attack on the press

- Type: Paraphrase from direct reading of local scans
- Source: Gutzkow, `Telegraph fuer Deutschland`, no. 7 (1839), p. 51
- Key wording:
  Journals share responsibility because review copies and financial dependency discourage frank criticism.
- Why it matters:
  This widens the target from publishers alone to the whole literary public sphere.

### 2.6 Named firms

- Type: Direct names from local scans
- Source: Gutzkow, `Telegraph fuer Deutschland`, no. 7, p. 51; no. 8, pp. 57-59
- Key wording:
  `J. J. Weber in Leipzig`
  `Ph. Reclam in Leipzig`
  `Vieweg in Braunschweig`
  `G. Westermann in Braunschweig`
  `Meyer senior in Braunschweig`
  `Mayer in Aachen`
  `Metzler'sche Buchhandlung in Stuttgart`
  `Kollmann in Leipzig`
- Why it matters:
  This is one of the best contemporary lists of firms treated as translation factories.

### 2.7 Machine language

- Type: Direct wording from local scan
- Source: Gutzkow, `Telegraph fuer Deutschland`, no. 8 (1839), p. 59
- Key wording:
  `Uebersetzungs-Maschinen`
- Associated names:
  `Gustav Pfizer`
  `Notter`
- Why it matters:
  This is one of the clearest direct links between translation labor and machine metaphor.

### 2.8 Scale estimate

- Type: Paraphrase from direct reading of local scans
- Source: Gutzkow, `Telegraph fuer Deutschland`, no. 8 (1839), p. 59
- Key wording:
  Eliminating the named firms would create an annual deficit of more than `150` entertainment books.
- Why it matters:
  This is a rare contemporary attempt to quantify the scale of the trade.

### 2.9 Kollmann as a complicated case

- Type: Paraphrase from direct reading of local scans
- Source: Gutzkow, `Telegraph fuer Deutschland`, no. 8 (1839), p. 59
- Key wording:
  Kollmann may be `vielleicht unschuldig`, because his business has only partly been made into a translation factory.
- Associated names:
  `Fanny Tarnow`
  `Kruse`
  `Andre`
- Why it matters:
  This complicates any flat blacklist model and suggests varied publisher positions within the same market.

## 3. Scott and the race to market

### 3.1 Advance sheets and speed

- Type: Paraphrase from primary letter tradition
- Source: Hauff correspondence via Bachleitner and Guentter
- Key wording:
  Franckh had arranged to be informed `14 Tage vor Erscheinen` and to receive advance sheets from London.
- Why it matters:
  This is a concrete mechanism of the race-to-market system.

### 3.2 The review complaint about speed

- Type: Reported wording from Bachleitner's summary of early criticism
- Source: `Blaetter fuer literarische Unterhaltung` (1823), via Bachleitner
- Key wording:
  The fastest translator is paid best, the most conscientious worst.
- Why it matters:
  This is the clearest compact statement of the incentive structure.

## 4. Translation comparison: strongest textual case

### 4.1 Scott, *The Bride of Lammermoor*

- Type: Direct original wording
- Source: Walter Scott, *The Bride of Lammermoor*
- Key wording:
  `the ingenious manager of Punch and his wife Joan`
- Why it matters:
  This is the source phrase behind the strongest current factory-style comparison.

- Type: Reported wording
- Source: `Die teutschen Uebersetzungen des Walter Scott` (1826), via Bachleitner and the Vienna script
- Gerhard rendering:
  `gleich dem genialen Punschwirth und seinem Weibe Johanna`
- Schumann / Montenglaut rendering:
  `gleich dem erfinderischen Punschwirth mit seinem Weibe Johanna`
- More careful comparator:
  `der sinnreiche Leiter von Pulcinell ... und seinem Weibe Johanna`
- Why it matters:
  Two rival German versions repeat nearly the same strange error, which strongly suggests contamination, borrowing, or rushed reuse.

### 4.2 Best writing formulation for the Scott case

- Type: Working prose for paper drafting
- Suggested line:
  One sign of factory translation is not merely bad style, but the recurrence of the same strange solution across rival editions.
- Why it matters:
  This is the cleanest way to frame the evidence in analytical prose.

## 5. Translation comparison: control case

### 5.1 Scott, *A Legend of Montrose*, Annot Lyle song

- Type: Direct original wording
- Source: Walter Scott, *A Legend of Montrose*
- Key wording:
  `Were I but free, I'd follow thee`

- Type: Direct translated wording now in hand
- Lindau version:
  `Waerst Du bei mir im Lebensthal ...`
- Sophie May version:
  `Waerst du wie ich im niedern Thal geboren ...`
- Why it matters:
  This is a useful control case showing that contemporaneous variation does not automatically equal factory-style degradation.

## 6. Translator labor as drudgery

### 6.1 Immermann

- Type: Direct quotation reported through Bachleitner
- Source: Karl Immermann letters
- Key wording:
  `opus infaustum`
- Why it matters:
  This is a compact expression of translation as burdensome wage labor.

### 6.2 Bauernfeld

- Type: Direct quotation tradition reported through Bachleitner
- Source: Eduard von Bauernfeld diaries and memoirs
- Key wording:
  `Robot`
- Why it matters:
  This helps connect translation to the language of repetitive labor rather than literary vocation.

## 7. Modern-connection language already supported by the evidence

- Type: Working synthesis line
- Suggested wording:
  Translation factories did not merely translate quickly; they generated recognizable textual signatures of industrialized production.
- Why it matters:
  This is one of the strongest overarching claims now supported by the evidence base.
